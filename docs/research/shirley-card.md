# The Shirley Card: Calibration as Invisible Infrastructure

How a reference photograph of a white woman named Shirley became the default
against which all skin was measured -- and what that tells us about whose
reality gets encoded into the systems we build.

---

## 1. Origin and Mechanics

### What a Shirley Card Was

In the 1940s and 1950s, Kodak developed a color calibration system for photo
printing labs. The system used a reference photograph -- a portrait of a white
woman surrounded by color blocks -- which was included in a corner of one of
the 36 frames on a film roll. Lab technicians would print the reference card on
their machines and compare the output against the calibrated version. If
Shirley's skin looked right, the print was correct. If her skin was off, the
machine needed adjustment.

The first model was Shirley Page, a Kodak employee. Her name became the generic
term. Every subsequent reference model, regardless of identity, was called
"Shirley." The card itself was labeled "normal."

### How Film Chemistry Encoded the Bias

Color film emulsions were chemically optimized to render light skin tones
accurately. The development chemicals that brought out brown, red, and yellow
tones were not prioritized. This was not a limitation of physics. It was a
design decision. The film's dynamic range -- the spectrum of light values it
could capture -- was tuned narrowly around the reflectance properties of
Caucasian skin.

The consequence was structural, not incidental: darker skin tones lost facial
contour, detail, and differentiation. In group photographs -- school portraits,
for example -- lighter-skinned subjects would be properly exposed while
darker-skinned subjects appeared as featureless silhouettes. The camera did not
fail to capture them. The camera was never designed to capture them. The
reference standard against which "correct" was defined excluded them from the
definition.

Earl Kage, former head of Kodak's Color Photo Studios, told researcher Lorna
Roth that he received complaints from families unhappy with the quality of
graduation photos in Rochester, New York, in the 1950s. These photos often
featured mixed-race groups of friends, and the darker children had no visible
facial contours or particularities in the resulting images.

### The Television Equivalent

The same pattern ran through television. In analog TV production, the final
step of camera color balancing required a live person -- called the "Colour
Girl" -- to stand in front of the cameras while engineers adjusted flesh-tone
rendering. Jan Kasoff, former NBC color-television cameraman on Saturday Night
Live, told Lorna Roth: "A good video engineer would have a Colour Girl stand
in front of the cameras and stay there while the cameramen lined up and focused
on her flesh tones to do their fine adjustments of matching and balancing the
cameras colorwise."

The Colour Girl was always white.

When Whoopi Goldberg, a dark-skinned actress, appeared on camera with Barbara
Walters, a pale-skinned interviewer, the two could not be color-calibrated with
the same camera settings. For a period, each had to be shot and
color-balanced with a separate camera, then electronically merged in the
control room. The technology was not neutral. It was calibrated to one body
and could not hold both.

In film production, the equivalent reference was called a "China Girl" (or
"Doll" or "Girl Head") -- a brief image of a white woman spliced into the
leader of a film print for lab technicians to use during color timing. Same
function. Same assumption. Same invisible standard.

## 2. The Timeline

### 1940s-1950s: Establishment

Kodak's Shirley card system was developed in the 1940s and mass-produced
through the 1950s, when Kodak produced nearly all color film sold in the
United States. The reference model was white. The card was labeled "normal."
The assumption was invisible because it was total.

### 1950s-1960s: Complaints

Earl Kage's account places the first complaints in the 1950s: families in
Rochester objecting to graduation photos that rendered their children as
shadows. But complaints from families did not move Kodak's product development.

### 1960s-1970s: Commercial Pressure (Not Civil Rights)

What finally prompted Kodak to reformulate was commercial pressure from two
industries that had nothing to do with racial justice:

- **Furniture manufacturers** complained that Kodak's film could not
  differentiate between light brown and dark brown wood tones -- cherry versus
  maple was indistinguishable in product photography.
- **Chocolate companies** complained that the film could not render the
  difference between milk chocolate and dark chocolate in advertisements.

The economic interests of furniture and confectionery companies accomplished
what decades of complaints from Black families could not. The market for
accurately rendering brown objects drove the reformulation. Accurately
rendering brown people was a side effect.

Jim Lyon, a photographer who joined Kodak's photo tech division and research
laboratories in the 1970s, was among the first to use Black models for Shirley
cards. His stated motivation was not political: "I wasn't trying to be
politically correct. I just wanted a superior product that was capable of
handling any color on Earth."

### 1995-1996: The Multiracial Shirley Card

In 1995, Kodak introduced a multiracial Shirley card featuring three women:
an Asian woman, a white woman, and a Black woman, each with different skin
tones and clothing colors. A Hispanic woman was added sometime after. Until
this point, all Shirley cards had featured white women exclusively and had
been labeled "normal."

This took approximately 45 years.

## 3. Lorna Roth's Research

### The Apparatuses of Visual Reproduction

Lorna Roth, professor at Concordia University in Montreal, published "Looking
at Shirley, the Ultimate Norm: Colour Balance, Image Technologies, and
Cognitive Equity" in the *Canadian Journal of Communication* (2009). Her
central contribution was locating racial bias not in front of the camera
(the subject), not behind the camera (the photographer), but *inside* the
camera -- in the film emulsions, the chemical processes, the calibration
standards, the reference cards. The bias was in the apparatus itself.

Roth introduced three theoretical categories for understanding how this bias
operated:

1. **The technological unconscious** (drawing on Franco Vaccari, 1981): the
   idea that technologies carry embedded assumptions that operate below the
   threshold of awareness. The Shirley card was not perceived as encoding a
   racial norm. It was perceived as encoding "correct" color.

2. **Dysconsciousness** (drawing on Joyce King, 2001): a form of consciousness
   that accepts existing inequities as given, natural, or inevitable. Not
   malice. Not ignorance. An uncritical habit of mind that accepts the
   dominant order.

3. **Cognitive equity** (Roth's original concept): an active strategy for
   inscribing a wider dynamic range of skin tones into image technologies,
   products, and practices. Not representation in front of the camera.
   Representation inside the technology.

### The Structural Insight

Roth's work demonstrated that the people producing the Shirley cards "had a
particular image of beauty" and that "at the time, in the 1950s, the people
who were buying cameras were mostly Caucasian people," so the industry
"didn't see the need for the market to expand to a broader range of skin
tones." The calibration standard was not the result of a conscious decision to
exclude. It was the result of a design process that never included -- which is
the same thing, structurally, when the product then defines what "normal"
looks like for everyone.

## 4. Precursors: Douglass and Du Bois

### Frederick Douglass (1818-1895)

Frederick Douglass was the most photographed American of the 19th century.
More than 160 distinct photographs and portraits of him survive -- more than
the 126 known portraits of Abraham Lincoln. This was not vanity. It was
strategy.

Douglass recognized photography as what he called "the great democratic art."
He praised Louis Daguerre as "the great discoverer of modern times" and
observed that "what was once the special and exclusive luxury of the rich and
great is now the privilege of all. The humblest servant girl may now possess a
picture of herself such as the wealth of kings could not purchase fifty years
ago."

But Douglass understood that the technology's democratic potential was
conditional on who controlled the image. In the April 7, 1849 edition of his
newspaper *The North Star*, he wrote:

> "Negroes can never have impartial portraits at the hands of white artists.
> It seems to us next to impossible for white men to take likenesses of black
> men, without most grossly exaggerating their distinctive features. And the
> reason is obvious. Artists, like all other white persons, have adopted a
> theory respecting the distinctive features of Negro physiognomy."

He identified the mechanism precisely: not individual prejudice but a
*theory* -- a cognitive framework -- that preceded the act of representation
and distorted it from within. The theory about what Black features looked
like shaped what white artists saw when they looked. The distortion was not
in the hand. It was in the eye.

Douglass specifically refused to smile in photographs. Not because he was
humorless, but because the smiling Black person was a weapon in the
pro-slavery iconography -- the "happy slave" who validated the institution.
His gravity was a counter-representation: a refusal to provide the image the
dominant narrative needed.

He delivered four speeches on photography throughout his life -- more than any
other American public figure of his era. He understood, a century before the
Shirley card, that the instrument of representation carries the politics of
representation inside it.

### W.E.B. Du Bois (1868-1963)

Du Bois continued the line Douglass opened. As editor of *The Crisis*, the
NAACP's magazine, from 1910 to 1934, Du Bois was deliberate about visual
representation. Images in *The Crisis* were almost entirely created by Black
artists and photographers, producing portraits that were "dignified,
respectful, and exuded race-pride" rather than reproducing the caricatures
that dominated white-controlled media.

In "Criteria of Negro Art" (1926), Du Bois articulated the structural
argument: white artists were constrained by the representational frameworks
of the dominant culture, so that even sympathetic white depictions of Black
life operated within a field of distortion that white artists could not see
from inside.

Du Bois's use of photography at the 1900 Paris Exposition -- where he
curated hundreds of photographs of African Americans alongside data
visualizations -- was an early act of what Roth would later call cognitive
equity: inscribing a wider range of human reality into the visual record.

The line from Douglass through Du Bois to the Shirley card is direct: who
controls the calibration standard controls whose reality is visible.

## 5. Digital Continuation

### The Pattern Transfers

When photography went digital, the calibration bias did not disappear. It
migrated. Digital cameras, video systems, and image-processing algorithms
inherited the same assumptions -- optimizing for lighter skin tones in
automatic exposure, white balance, and face detection.

### Joy Buolamwini and the Aspire Mirror

As an undergraduate at Georgia Institute of Technology, Joy Buolamwini was
building an art project called the Aspire Mirror -- a facial-recognition
system that would reflect the face of someone who inspired the user onto the
user's own face. Buolamwini, a dark-skinned Black woman, anticipated seeing
Serena Williams reflected back at her.

The system could not detect her face.

It detected her lighter-skinned colleagues almost instantaneously. It detected
Buolamwini only when she put on a white mask. She took the mask off and
disappeared.

The Shirley card, digitized.

### Gender Shades (2018)

Buolamwini pursued this discovery at MIT Media Lab, collaborating with
Timnit Gebru (then at Microsoft Research) to produce "Gender Shades:
Intersectional Accuracy Disparities in Commercial Gender Classification,"
published in the *Proceedings of the 1st Conference on Fairness,
Accountability and Transparency* (2018).

**The benchmark:** They constructed the Pilot Parliaments Benchmark (PPB) --
1,270 faces of parliamentarians from three African countries and three
European countries, creating a dataset distributed 53.6% lighter-skinned
and 46.4% darker-skinned. This was a deliberate corrective to existing
benchmarks: IJB-A was 79.6% lighter-skinned; Adience was 86.2%
lighter-skinned. The benchmarks that were used to validate the systems
reproduced the same exclusion as the Shirley card.

**The systems audited:** IBM, Microsoft, and Face++ (Megvii).

**The findings:**

| Subgroup | Best Error Rate | Worst Error Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Lighter-skinned males | 0.8% | -- |
| Darker-skinned females | -- | 34.7% |

All three companies performed better on males than females (8.1%--20.6%
difference). All performed better on lighter subjects than darker subjects
(11.8%--19.2% difference). The worst performance in every system was on
darker-skinned females.

IBM's gap was the largest: 34.4 percentage points between lighter males and
darker females. Microsoft misgendered darker subjects in 93.6% of its
errors. Face++ misgendered women in 95.9% of its errors.

Overall error rates: IBM 12.1%, Face++ 9.9%, Microsoft 6.2%. But these
aggregated numbers hid the intersectional disparity. The system that was
"94% accurate overall" was 34.7% wrong on the people who needed accuracy
most.

**The response:** IBM and Microsoft both improved their systems after the
paper's publication. IBM eventually exited the facial recognition business
entirely, citing concerns about mass surveillance and racial profiling.

### The Broader Pattern

Buolamwini's discovery -- that a white mask made her visible to a machine --
was the Shirley card in miniature. The calibration standard was inside the
technology. Not in the lighting. Not in the photographer. In the training
data, the loss functions, the benchmarks, the optimization targets. The
system was calibrated to one body and could not see the others.

## 6. Google Photos (2015)

On June 29, 2015, Jacky Alcine, a Black software developer in Brooklyn, New
York, tweeted a screenshot of Google's Photos app. The app had automatically
tagged photographs of him and a Black female friend under the label
"Gorillas."

Yonatan Zunger, then Google's Chief Architect of Social, responded within
approximately 90 minutes: "Can we have your permission to examine the data in
your account in order to figure out how this happened?" Within hours, Zunger
said a fix was in the works.

### The "Fix"

Google's immediate solution was to remove the label "gorillas" from all
photos -- including photos of actual gorillas. It was described as a
temporary patch while a more robust solution was developed.

Three years later, the robust solution had not been developed.

In January 2018, *Wired* journalist Tom Simonite tested Google Photos with
a collection of 40,000 images well-stocked with animals. The service
identified pandas, poodles, and many other creatures. But it returned "no
results" for the search terms "gorilla," "chimp," "chimpanzee," and "monkey."

A Google spokesperson confirmed to *Wired* that "gorilla" had been censored
from searches and image tags since the 2015 incident. "Chimp,"
"chimpanzee," and "monkey" were also blocked. The spokesperson wrote: "Image
labeling technology is still early and unfortunately it's nowhere near
perfect."

By 2023, the labels were *still* removed.

Google's response to a system that could not distinguish Black people from
primates was not to fix the system's ability to see Black people. It was to
remove the system's ability to see primates. The calibration failure was
papered over, not repaired. The system remained unable to see what it had
never been calibrated to see.

## 7. The Structural Insight

### Calibration as Invisible Infrastructure

Margaret Visser, in *Much Depends on Dinner* (1986), wrote:

> "The extent to which we take everyday objects for granted is the precise
> extent to which they govern and inform our lives."

The Shirley card is the paradigmatic case. A reference photograph in a photo
lab -- something no consumer ever saw, something most photographers never
thought about -- defined whose skin was "normal." That definition propagated
through film chemistry, development processes, printing equipment, television
cameras, and eventually into digital systems, training datasets, and machine
learning benchmarks. Each layer inherited the assumption and passed it
forward. Each layer appeared neutral. The bias was invisible precisely because
it was infrastructure.

### What the Shirley Card Teaches

The Shirley card is not primarily a story about racism in technology, though
it is that. It is a story about how *calibration choices become invisible
once they become infrastructure*. The choice of whose skin defines "normal"
disappeared into the chemistry of the film, the design of the sensor, the
composition of the training dataset. Once there, it could not be seen by the
people using the technology, because seeing it would require stepping outside
the frame the technology provided -- and the technology was the frame.

Lorna Roth's concept of the "technological unconscious" names this precisely:
assumptions embedded so deeply in a technology that they operate below the
threshold of awareness. The Shirley card was not a policy. It was not a
decision that could be reversed by a different decision-maker. It was the
default. And defaults, once established, reproduce themselves through every
system that inherits them.

The line from Douglass to Du Bois to the Shirley card to Buolamwini is the
line from a *theory* about what Black faces look like (Douglass, 1849), to
that theory encoded in institutions of visual representation (Du Bois,
1900-1926), to that theory materialized in film chemistry and calibration
standards (Kodak, 1940s-1990s), to that theory inherited by machine learning
systems that were never explicitly taught it (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018).

Each generation of technology inherited the bias from the previous generation's
calibration choices. Not because anyone intended to transmit it. Because no
one intended not to.
